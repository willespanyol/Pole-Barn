Pole Barn Permit Plans: The Complete Drawing Checklist for 2026

A building permit for a pole barn usually takes more than a basic floor plan. The reviewer needs to see where the building sits, what supports the roof, how wind and snow forces reach the ground, and how the chosen details meet the locally adopted building code. A complete, coordinated drawing set answers those questions before construction begins.

Whether you're building a 30 × 40-ft equipment shed, a 40 × 60-ft workshop or a residential barndominium, this guide shows the documentation to gather and the drawings to request. The exact submission rules are set by the building authority for the project address, so confirm them before ordering the final set.

Do you need a building permit for a pole barn?

The answer depends on the jurisdiction, zoning classification, building use, area and construction scope. An agricultural building may have a different exemption or review pathway from a residential garage, commercial storage structure or a barndominium with occupied living space. Do not assume that calling a building a “farm barn” removes structural design requirements or makes it exempt.

Before paying for drawings, contact both the local building department and the planning/zoning office. Ask for the applicable code edition, required drawing sheets, site setbacks, wind and snow design criteria, engineering-seal requirements, and whether the application is submitted through an online portal. A request for a zoning clearance can be separate from a building permit.

For contractors: Obtain the actual permit checklist or correction letter, not just a verbal “plans required.” Attach that document to your drawing request so the design team can align the sheets with the reviewer’s submission format.

Structural items that deserve engineering attention

1. Local wind, snow, rain and seismic criteria

Post-frame buildings use long columns and relatively widely spaced structural members. A roof that spans well under gravity load may still need significant lateral bracing and uplift resistance under wind. The engineer should identify site-specific design criteria from the adopted code and applicable referenced standard, rather than copying a generic wind-speed value from another county.

ASCE/SEI 7-22 addresses the loads and combinations used in US structural engineering, including wind, snow, rain and seismic effects. The building code edition adopted by the local authority controls which version and amendments apply.

2. Continuous load paths

A useful way to read a framed building is to follow forces from roof cladding through purlins, trusses or rafters, posts and foundations to the supporting soil. Separately, follow the lateral load route through roof or wall diaphragms, bracing, frames and anchorage. If a section draws a brace without showing what it connects to, the drawing may not be complete enough for construction or plan review.

The National Frame Building Association’s technical resources cover post-frame diaphragm and lateral system design. Connection and load path assumptions should be documented as part of the engineered design.

3. Foundations are not a generic detail

Embedded posts, cast-in-place concrete piers, surface-mounted columns and perimeter foundations can behave differently under uplift, overturning and lateral loads. A complete foundation plan should identify dimensions, embedment, soil assumptions, frost protection where relevant, reinforcement or anchors, and the connection between post and footing. Avoid selecting footing dimensions solely from a typical detail found online.

4. Truss coordination and opening sizes

Large vehicle or equipment doors alter framing and lateral resistance. Confirm the clear opening width, required header and jamb arrangement, overhead door headroom, truss bearing positions and any manufacturer-supplied truss engineering. Truss drawings need to match the actual roof loading and support layout shown on the architectural set.

When should you request a licensed engineer’s stamp?

Many owners need signed and sealed drawings because their building department specifically requests them, the post-frame system requires engineering, or the project is being submitted for commercial or occupied use. Ask the permit office what seal and signature form it accepts. The professional responsible for engineering review must hold appropriate authorization for the project’s jurisdiction.

Five avoidable reasons permit sets get returned

  1. Generic design loads: The wind and snow criteria on the drawings are inconsistent with the permit location.
  2. Missing load path: The roofing, bracing and foundations are detailed individually but the lateral or uplift forces are not accounted for continuously.
  3. Conflicting dimensions: Door locations, column spacing or eave heights differ among the floor plan, sections and structural sheets.
  4. No verified site information: The building footprint looks correct but setbacks, easements, drainage or property lines are missing.
  5. Incomplete professional documentation: The sealing requirements, truss engineering or response to previous review comments are omitted.

To reduce revisions, freeze the key dimensions before calculations, match all sheet references to the same revision date, run an interdisciplinary drawing review and check the complete package against the actual city or county checklist.

Pole barn permit plan FAQ

Can I get pole barn plans without having a full survey?

Often you can start design with a site sketch, but the building department may require a formal survey, dimensioned site plan or verified setbacks before submission. Ask the local office early.

Is a supplier’s kit drawing the same as a permit set?

Not necessarily. A material list or kit layout may not include the site-specific load criteria, foundation engineering, complete connections, elevations or sealed documentation requested for permit review.

Do I need different drawings for a barndominium?

Usually yes. Living areas introduce occupancy, egress, thermal-envelope, electrical, plumbing, HVAC and other requirements. A simple equipment-storage plan does not adequately describe an occupied residential building.
